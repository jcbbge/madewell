# Challenging Assumptions

Constructive opposition is not a long list of things that might go wrong. Find the **hinge**: the premise on which the plan's advantage or acceptability actually depends.

## Preserve the strongest honest case

Name the goal and why the proposal could meet it. Separate supplied facts from the proponent's estimates and claims; steel-manning does not convert "we expect savings" into "we have proved savings." Preserve the user's goals, constraints, and authority.

Extract the dependencies behind the recommendation: required demand, capacity, compatibility, behavior, timing, ownership, or recovery. Ask of each: **If this were false, would we choose differently?** If not, it is not the decision's hinge, even if it is interesting.

## Challenge a dependency, not the person

For each material hinge, connect:

**Assumption → way it could fail → consequence for the goal → evidence that would distinguish the cases.**

Look for a boundary case, a changed operating condition, a missing owner, or correlated failures that defeat an apparently independent backup. Examine the negative case as carefully as the positive case. A single plausible failure story is a hypothesis, not an estimated probability.

A premortem or inversion can generate candidates: imagine failure, then ask which observable mechanism could produce it. Return from the imagined future to present evidence before treating any story as a finding. Do not use vividness, named experts, or agreement among imagined personas as corroboration.

Separate known fatal constraints, untested consequential assumptions, and manageable costs. Do not assign numerical risk scores or probabilities without a defensible basis. A heuristic score can organize discussion if explicitly labeled; it cannot measure risk or justify trading away a safety boundary.

## Convert criticism into a decision

Recommend the cheapest authorized observation or test that could change the recommendation: a recovery drill in a safe environment, a representative workload, an actual willingness-to-pay signal, or inspection of the relevant contract. Explain what different results would imply.

For a reversible low-consequence experiment, a focused failure check and rollback condition may be enough. For consequential irreversible action, unverified guarantees remain unresolved. Do not create a weekly review process, an exhaustive framework exercise, or a new approval gate merely because this skill was invoked.

Keep challenges that change the decision, mitigation, or evidence required. Drop objections already answered by credible evidence. If the plan survives the meaningful challenge, say what was tested and what remains uncertain; do not invent a fatal flaw to justify the role.

Questions answerable from context, artifacts, or a safe test belong to the agent. Ask the user only for genuinely missing intent or authority. End with a supported recommendation, not "here are all the risks; you decide."

## Deliver

Give the strongest case, the material hinge, the failure mechanism and evidence status, and the next test or revised recommendation. Name the cost of mitigation as well as the risk it addresses. Keep the critique about the plan, not a diagnosis of its author.
